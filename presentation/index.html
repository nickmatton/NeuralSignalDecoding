Why neural decoding

Intracortical BCIs read motor cortex to restore movement and control for people with paralysis — moving a cursor, a robotic arm, or a reanimated limb.

The decoder is the core ML component: it turns neural activity into intended movement, in real time, continuously.

This project asks a focused question:

Given the same data and training budget, which decoder architecture best maps spikes → hand kinematics, and what drives the difference?

Frame it as an applied, real-time regression problem. The architecture comparison is the contribution — controlled, same data, same training recipe.

The task

At each 50 ms time bin, predict the hand kinematics from neural activity:

95 spike counts
(+ recent history) → decoder → X / Y position
X / Y velocity
  • Supervised regression, 4 continuous targets.
  • Metric: Pearson correlation r between predicted and actual, per target, then averaged. 1.0 = perfect tracking.
r is the standard BCI decoding metric — scale-invariant, interpretable. I report per-target and the average. Later I'll argue we should also report R²/RMSE.

The data

  • contdata95.mat — 31,413 contiguous time bins @ 50 ms ≈ 26 minutes of recording
  • Inputs: 95 channels of integer spike counts (0–21 per bin) — sparse, discrete, noisy
  • Targets: 4 kinematics — X/Y position and X/Y velocity of the hand

Spikes are the input modality — not ECoG/voltages. Note the dynamic-range gap: velocity swings are large; position is comparatively small and smooth. Keep that in mind for the results.

What makes it hard (and easy)

Hard:

  • Spike counts are sparse & noisy, 95-dimensional
  • Mapping to smooth kinematics is nonlinear and temporal

Easy (be careful):

  • It's a reaching task — rest near center, reach, return
  • Position is slowly varying / highly autocorrelated → easy to track once you have velocity
  • Neighboring bins are nearly identical

These "easy" properties inflate correlation — we'll return to this when reading the results.

This slide sets up the honesty discussion. High r is partly real skill, partly the data being autocorrelated. Flag it early so the big numbers later are credible, not oversold.

Preprocessing & splits

  • Temporal 70 / 15 / 15 split — train = first 70% of time, then val, then test. Never shuffled: adjacent bins are correlated, so random splitting would leak the future into training.
  • Z-score both the spike counts and the kinematic targets using training statistics only — normalizing the targets balances the loss across position & velocity (no peeking at val/test).
  • Context windows: sequence models see a 32-bin (1.6 s) sliding window; the MLP sees a single bin.

I verified the windowing introduces no target leakage across the split boundaries.

The temporal split is the single most important methodological choice for time-series neural data. Emphasize: leakage is the classic way to get fake-good decoding numbers; we avoided it and checked.

Four architectures — a spectrum of how they use time

ModelTemporal contextCore idea
MLPnone (1 bin)fully-connected baseline
2D CNN32-bin windowwindow as an image; local spatio-temporal filters
LSTM32-bin windowrecurrent memory over the sequence
Transformer32-bin windowself-attention over the sequence

Same inputs, same targets, same training recipe — so differences are architectural.

The organizing principle of the whole comparison: how much, and how, each model exploits temporal structure. That axis explains most of the results.

1 · MLP — single-bin baseline

95→ FC 64→ FC 64→ FC 128→ FC 128→ FC 256→ 4
  • Five hidden fully-connected layers; BatchNorm + ReLU + Dropout 0.3 after each.
  • Decodes from a single 50 ms bin — no temporal context at all.
  • Purpose: a strong, cheap baseline. How far can instantaneous firing alone get you?
Deliberately simple. It's the control: anything a sequence model gains over this is the value of temporal context. Spoiler — the MLP is already quite good, which is itself informative.

2 · 2D CNN — window as an image

1×32×95→ Conv 32, k5→ Conv 64, k5→ Conv 128, k3→ Conv 128, k3→ GAP→ FC 64→ 4
  • Treats the 32 × 95 (time × channel) window as a grayscale image.
  • Four conv blocks (BN + ReLU + Dropout 0.3), then global average pooling → FC head.
  • Captures local spatio-temporal patterns; pooling makes it translation-tolerant.

Note: global average pooling discards absolute position information — remember this for the results.

The GAP choice foreshadows the result: great for velocity (local, translation-invariant) but it throws away where-in-the-window things happen, which hurts absolute position.

3 · LSTM — recurrent memory

32×95→ BatchNorm→ LSTM 128→ final hidden state→ FC→ 4
  • Processes the 32-bin sequence one step at a time, carrying a 128-d hidden state.
  • Predicts from the final hidden state — a learned summary of 1.6 s of history.
  • Recurrence is a natural fit for smoothly evolving kinematics.
The classic sequence model for BCI. Recurrent state integrates evidence over time — well-matched to the smooth, momentum-like dynamics of reaching.

4 · Transformer — self-attention

32×95→ BatchNorm→ Linear 95→130→ + positional encoding→ Encoder ×2
(10 heads)→ mean-pool→ 4
  • Projects each bin to 130-d, then two encoder layers of multi-head self-attention.
  • Sinusoidal positional encoding is essential: attention is permutation-invariant, so without it the model can't tell when each bin occurred.
  • Mean-pools across the window → FC head.
Highlight positional encoding (yellow box). This is the key architectural detail and it shows up dramatically in the results: attention with no notion of order is nearly useless on a temporal signal.

Training protocol — identical for all four

  • Loss: MSE on the 4 z-scored targets
  • Optimizer: Adam, lr 1e-3
  • Weight decay 1e-4 — on weights only (not BatchNorm/bias)
  • LR schedule: ReduceLROnPlateau (×0.5, patience 5)
  • Grad clipping (norm 1.0) for LSTM / Transformer
  • Early stopping on val loss (patience 15)
  • ≤ 100 epochs, batch size 64
  • Best-by-val checkpoint restored before test
  • Everything seeded for reproducibility

Same recipe everywhere → the comparison isolates architecture, not tuning.

Fairness is the point: I did not hand-tune each model separately. A fixed, reasonable recipe for all four. That makes the architectural comparison clean.

The experiment: a multi-seed sweep

  • A single run can be lucky or unlucky — random init, data shuffling, and dropout all add variance.
  • So I trained every model across 5 random seeds and report mean ± std, not a single number.
  • Ran the 20 trainings on a Lambda A10 GPU. (The 2D CNN's convolutions are ~8 h on CPU but minutes on GPU.)

All seeds share the same temporal split — so this std measures optimization stability, which we'll interpret carefully later.

This is the rigor slide. Multi-seed mean±std is increasingly expected and avoids cherry-picking. Be honest now that it's seed variance on a fixed split — sets up the limitations section.

2D CNN — fixed by balancing the loss

With z-scored targets the CNN now tracks position too (~0.96), not just velocity — earlier it lagged on position (0.84 avg) only because velocity dominated the loss.

Earlier this slide was "the informative failure" — the CNN was weak on position. That turned out to be a loss-scaling artifact, not the architecture: position (±100) and velocity (±700) differ ~4× in scale, so an unweighted MSE was dominated by velocity. Z-scoring the four targets fixed it (0.84 → 0.96).

Insight 1 — loss balance rivaled architecture

  • Position (±100) and velocity (±700) differ ~4× in scale, so an unweighted MSE was dominated by velocity and starved position.
  • Z-scoring the four targets (equal weight in the loss) lifted the weaker models sharply: 2D CNN 0.84 → 0.96, MLP 0.88 → 0.93 — the CNN's "position weakness" was a loss artifact, not its architecture.
  • The sequence models barely moved (already near ceiling).

A preprocessing choice — not a fancier model — closed most of the gap. Correlation is scale-invariant, so the metric is unchanged; the trained model isn't.

The headline methodology takeaway, and an honest one: before this fix I'd have told a clean "sequence models win big" story. The real lesson is that a basic loss-balancing step mattered as much as the architecture for the non-recurrent models.

Insight 2 — positional encoding makes the Transformer work

Self-attention is permutation-invariant: with no positional signal, the encoder sees the 32-bin window as an unordered set.

  • No positional encoding → r ≈ 0.74
  • + sinusoidal positional encoding → r = 0.985

It's an architectural necessity, not a tuning knob: a temporal signal demands that the model know when each input occurred.

With it, the Transformer essentially matches the LSTM.

Frame as architecture, not a "bug fix." The lesson generalizes: any attention model on sequential data needs positional information, or it's blind to order.

Insight 3 — temporal context still helps, but modestly

  • With the loss balanced, the ordering is LSTM 0.989 ≳ Transformer 0.985 > 2D CNN 0.960 > MLP 0.928 — sequence models lead, but only by a few points.
  • A single 50 ms bin (MLP, no context at all) already reaches 0.93 on this session.
  • Seed variance is tiny (±0.000–0.004), so the ranking is stable.

That small margin is itself a clue: this single autocorrelated session is "easy". The real test of whether context matters is a harder benchmark — next slide.

Honest reframing: temporal context helps but isn't the dramatic lever it looked like before the loss fix. A single-bin MLP at 0.93 says the session is easy/autocorrelated — which sets up the NLB validation where the spread between models, and the role of context, is clearer.

Reading the numbers honestly

  • Why r ≈ 0.98 isn't as wild as it looks: position is slowly varying / autocorrelated, and stride-1 windows make adjacent samples near-duplicates — both inflate correlation.
  • No leakage: verified the temporal split has no target overlap across boundaries.
  • What the std does and doesn't say: all seeds share one split, so std measures optimization stability, not generalization. The LSTM's ±0.000 is the tell — stable training, not zero uncertainty about a new session.
The credibility slide. Volunteering the caveats is what makes the strong numbers believable. Knowing WHY a metric looks good is as important as the metric.

Validated on a public benchmark

To check the decoders are genuinely good — not just riding one session's autocorrelation — I re-ran the two sequence models on the Neural Latents Benchmark: standardized datasets (NWB / DANDI), official held-out split, metric = velocity R², two different monkeys.

Limitations & next steps

  • Done: validated on a public benchmark (NLB, 2 subjects) with R² — addressing the single-session and metric concerns.
  • Still within-session. Even NLB holds out trials from the same session → next is cross-day / cross-subject transfer (e.g. the FALCON benchmark).
  • Seed-std ≠ data variance. Add blocked / time-series cross-validation to capture data uncertainty.
  • Add classical baselines (Wiener / Kalman filter) — the standard reference decoders in BCI.
Frame as a trajectory: I already closed the two biggest gaps (one session, R²) via NLB. The remaining asks — cross-day transfer, blocked CV, Kalman baselines — are the natural next steps a PI would request.

Takeaways

  • Preprocessing rivals architecture — z-scoring the targets to balance the loss lifted the 2D CNN 0.84→0.96 and the MLP 0.88→0.93; sequence models (LSTM 0.989, Transformer 0.985) lead only modestly.
  • Architecture details matter — the Transformer needs positional encoding to use time at all.
  • A single 50 ms bin is a strong baseline — the MLP reaches 0.93 on this session.
  • It generalizes: on the public Neural Latents Benchmark (2 monkeys, velocity R²) the LSTM leads — RTT 0.61, Maze 0.86 — not just one easy session.
  • Rigor: fixed recipe, seeded, 5-seed mean ± std, leakage-checked (incl. a fixed early-stop leak), benchmark-validated.
